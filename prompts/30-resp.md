## PART B

### [Q1-reading-level] Vocabulary / reading level and instruction complexity

- **Kids 4-8** (spans pre-reader to early-reader): Assume many readers can't yet read instructions independently. Use picture-led or icon-based instructions ("look → do") backed by a single short imperative sentence a parent can read aloud (e.g., "Color the sun yellow"). Avoid multi-step or conditional instructions ("if you finish early, then..."). By the top of the band (7-8), short full sentences with sight-word vocabulary are workable.
- **Kids 6-10**: Independent readers at grade-appropriate level (roughly grade 1-5). Instructions can be one or two short sentences, but avoid compound/conditional logic and abstract vocabulary. Define any puzzle mechanic in plain language with a worked example rather than jargon like "diagonally adjacent."
- **Tween 8-12**: Comfortable with paragraph-length instructions and can follow a worked example once. This is the age where puzzle _mechanics_ can be introduced formally (e.g., sudoku's row/column/box rule, logic-grid deduction), provided a fully-solved example accompanies the first instance.
- **Teen 13-17**: Adult-level reading comprehension is typical; the constraint is not vocabulary but _content relevance_ (contemporary references, non-childish framing) and _tone_ (avoid instructions that read as condescending).
- **Adult**: Full adult vocabulary and instruction density; assume prior familiarity with common formats (sudoku, crossword) needs no re-explanation, but note niche formats (kakuro, nonograms/picture logic) still need a one-paragraph rules primer since many adults haven't encountered them.
- **Senior Large Print**: Vocabulary matches general adult level — large print is a _visual_ accommodation, not a cognitive simplification, and should never be designed as if it were a children's product. Instructions should stay concise per page since re-reading dense instruction blocks in large type consumes more visual scanning effort.

### [Q2-concept-load] Concept load & attention span

- **Kids 4-8**: Very short sittings (roughly 5-15 minutes depending on the low/high end of the band `unverified` exact figure, general publishing convention). Favor single-activity pages with heavy scaffolding (example shown, first item pre-filled/solved) and low activity count per sitting (1-3 pages).
- **Kids 6-10**: Moderate sittings (~15-25 minutes `unverified`). Introduce light difficulty progression across a book/series rather than mixing difficulty randomly, and offer a visible "you did it" marker per puzzle (checkbox, sticker prompt) to sustain motivation across a sitting.
- **Tween 8-12**: Can sustain single-topic focus for a full puzzle set in one sitting; this is the age to introduce book-level scaffolding (page 1 tutorial puzzle) rather than per-page hand-holding.
- **Teen 13-17**: Attention span is adult-comparable but engagement is reward/novelty-driven; vary puzzle difficulty and format within a book (mini-challenges, "bonus" puzzles) to sustain interest rather than uniform, repetitive difficulty.
- **Adult**: Widest range of use patterns — quick fill-ins during a commute vs. long focused sessions. Structuring a book so puzzles work equally well solved out of order supports this.
- **Senior Large Print**: Design for shorter sessions with easy re-entry (one puzzle per page, clear numbering, no dependency between puzzles) since large-type layouts mean fewer puzzles fit per sitting and readers may pause and resume more often.

### [Q3-motor] Fine-motor considerations

- **Kids 4-8**: This is the band most driven by fine-motor readiness. Coloring-book line weight should run roughly **5-8px (about 3-4mm)** for the youngest end, tapering toward 3-4px by age 6-7, per multiple coloring-book design guides. Maze paths should be wide (~1cm+) with minimal branching. Dot-to-dot spacing should be generous and numeral sequences capped low (1-20). Handwriting-practice lines should be extra-wide with directional arrows.
- **Kids 6-10**: Fine motor control matures quickly across this band; line weight can taper to roughly 2-4px, mazes can add branching complexity, and grid-based puzzles (junior sudoku, word search) become viable once a child can reliably write small characters in a bounded cell.
- **Senior Large Print**: The other motor-sensitive band, for a different reason — reduced grip strength, hand tremor, or arthritis can affect precision `unverified` prevalence details. Coloring-book line weight should be bumped back up to roughly **3-5px**, with larger open fill regions and avoidance of small enclosed cells. Puzzle grid cells (sudoku, crossword) should be sized generously (roughly 0.5in+ per cell) so a pen doesn't have to work inside a cramped box.
- **Tween/Teen/Adult**: Fine motor control is not a binding constraint for these bands; line weight and cell size become primarily aesthetic/difficulty choices (finer lines and denser detail signal "for grown-ups/experienced colorists" rather than serving a motor need).

### [Q4-accessibility] Accessibility norms

- **Kids 4-8 / 6-10**: No formal large-print standard applies, but "big and clear" is still a functional accessibility norm: bold sans-serif instruction text (roughly 14-18pt), one activity per page, high black-on-white contrast, and simple uncluttered layouts benefit early readers and any child with visual or attention differences, without needing to be labeled as an accessibility feature.
- **Tween 8-12 / Teen 13-17**: Standard trade-book typography is generally appropriate (body/instruction text ~11-14pt), but keep contrast high and avoid low-contrast novelty fonts on puzzle instructions specifically (decorative fonts are fine for cover/theming, not for anything that must be read to complete the puzzle).
- **Adult**: Standard adult puzzle-book/journal conventions apply — typically 10-12pt body text is the norm for general adult publishing, per publishing-industry data, though puzzle grids themselves are usually set larger than body text for legibility.
- **Senior Large Print**: This band has the most codified standards, and they come from several overlapping sources that don't perfectly agree, so treat the following as a defensible working range rather than one official number:
  - Retailer/POD minimums: Amazon KDP's large-print minimum is commonly cited as **16pt**; IngramSpark treats **14-18pt** as "large print" and **18pt+** as "giant print."
  - Advocacy-organization standards (American Council of the Blind, NAVH/Lighthouse International, American Printing House for the Blind) generally recommend starting at **18pt**, with **20pt** cited as a fuller "large print" standard by the American Council of the Blind — i.e. treat 18pt as a floor, not a target, when the goal is genuine accessibility rather than the bare retailer label.
  - WCAG 2.0 (web standard, useful as a cross-reference) defines "large text" as **18pt (24px)**.
  - Font choice: sans-serif (or a very open, humanist serif) is consistently recommended over decorative or heavily-serifed faces; avoid italics and all-caps for body/instruction text.
  - Line spacing (leading): roughly **1.5x** the font size is a commonly cited target for large-print body text.
  - Contrast/paper: high-contrast black text on white or cream matte paper; avoid glossy stock, which can introduce glare.
  - Layout: one puzzle/activity per page is the norm at this size, since larger type simply doesn't leave room for two.
  - Binding: lay-flat or spiral binding is frequently recommended so the book stays open without being held down — useful for anyone completing a puzzle one-handed.

### [Q5-safety-tone] Tone / imagery boundaries

- **Kids 4-8**: Content should be unambiguously friendly — familiar animals, everyday objects, gentle fantasy (no monsters framed as threatening, no implied danger/peril in maze or activity themes). Facial expressions on characters should read as clearly positive/neutral.
- **Kids 6-10**: Mild adventure and mystery framing is fine (space, dinosaurs, "spooky-but-silly" Halloween themes) as long as outcomes are safe and non-threatening; avoid real-world danger themes (weapons, violence, disaster).
- **Tween 8-12**: Fantasy/adventure content can include conflict-adjacent themes (dragons, quests) but should avoid graphic violence, gore, or genuinely frightening ambiguous-threat imagery; humor should avoid content that reads as mean-spirited or exclusionary.
- **Teen 13-17**: Broader tonal range is acceptable (moodier aesthetics like "dark academia," more mature vocabulary in crosswords/cryptograms) but should still avoid graphic violence, self-harm imagery, or content that normalizes risky behavior; avoid a tone that reads as talking down to teens.
- **Adult**: Widest latitude, but any wellness-adjacent product (mindfulness workbook, affirmation coloring, gratitude journal) should stay in functional/self-help framing rather than implying diagnostic or treatment claims.
- **Senior Large Print**: Dignity is the operative principle — avoid childish art styles, patronizing language ("just for fun, sweetie"), or framing that implies decline/incapacity. Reminiscence/life-story content should be framed as a positive legacy-writing activity, not around loss or memory difficulty. Imagery should be realistic/adult in style even when simplified for motor accessibility.

### [Q6-parent-buyer] Who actually buys, and what reassures them

- **Kids 4-8 / 6-10**: Primarily parents and grandparents, often buying as travel/quiet-time activities or as gifts. Reassurance cues: visible educational value (letters, counting, vocabulary), "screen-free" positioning, safety of materials/format (non-toxic implied, sturdy paper), and age-range clarity on the cover so the buyer isn't guessing at difficulty fit.
- **Tween 8-12**: Mixed — parents still buy directly, but tweens increasingly influence or make their own low-cost purchases (allowance, gift cards). Reassurance for parents leans on "screen-free" and "brain-building" framing; appeal to the tween themselves leans on theme/aesthetic (fantasy, trending characters-adjacent art, not licensed IP) and a sense of "this is for my age, not a little-kid book."
- **Teen 13-17**: Both direct teen purchases (with own money/gift cards) and parent/relative gift purchases occur. Parents buying for teens respond to "focus," "stress relief," and "screen-free downtime" framing; teens themselves respond to aesthetic/trend cues (cover design, dark academia/cottagecore-style art) more than to the activity type itself.
- **Adult**: Primarily self-purchase, plus a meaningful gift-market segment (holidays, Mother's/Father's Day, get-well gifts). Self-purchasers look for stated puzzle count/value and difficulty labeling; gift-buyers look for an appealing cover and a clear occasion-fit theme.
- **Senior Large Print**: A distinctive three-sided buyer market: (1) the senior buying for themselves, who cares most about genuine readability and dignity of design; (2) adult children/grandchildren buying as a gift, who are reassured by explicit "large print" labeling, a large-print sample image on the cover/listing, and content that doesn't look childish; and (3) institutional buyers (libraries, senior living facilities, care programs) who may buy in bulk and value durable/lay-flat binding and clearly stated font size on the packaging.
