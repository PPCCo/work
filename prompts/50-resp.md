## PART B

### [Q1-interior-motifs]

Functional motif tendencies by category/audience combination:

- **Young kids (4-8), coloring/mazes/dot-to-dot:** single dominant subject per page (one animal, one vehicle, one shape) rather than busy scenes; motifs chosen for instant recognizability at a glance rather than narrative complexity.
- **Kids 6-10, puzzle books:** motifs shift toward light "knowledge" framing — animals, geography, simple science facts — giving the puzzle a secondary educational hook rather than pure abstraction.
- **Tweens (8-12):** motifs move toward mild challenge/reward framing — puzzle "difficulty ramps," fantasy creature themes for coloring — signaling the product is a step up from younger books without being teen-coded.
- **Teens (13-17):** motifs lean toward personal identity and self-regulation themes (mood check-ins, symmetry/pattern calm) rather than childlike subjects; geometric and abstract motifs read as more age-appropriate than literal animals or characters.
- **Adults, puzzle books:** motif is largely absent by design — the grid/structure itself is the product; any illustration is decorative framing, not the point.
- **Adults, coloring:** botanical, floral, and geometric/mandala motifs dominate; motifs function as a vehicle for dense mark-making and a meditative/repetitive activity rather than as subject matter to be admired.
- **Adults, journals/planners:** motif is minimal to none; structure (grids, dividers, prompt hierarchy) carries the entire visual load.
- **Senior/large-print:** motifs skew toward nostalgia-adjacent, nature, and "everyday life" themes with deliberately uncluttered single-subject framing — chosen for legibility and familiarity, not novelty. `unverified` that nostalgia-themed word lists specifically outperform generic themes in this segment — plausible from general large-print publishing patterns but not something confirmed here.

### [Q2-line-density]

- **Kids 4-8:** thick, uniform outline weight throughout; very large open fill areas; near-zero interior line detail so a young colorist/solver isn't overwhelmed.
- **Kids 6-10:** slightly thinner outlines than the 4-8 tier, first appearance of small secondary detail zones (a texture patch, a background element), but still low overall density.
- **Tweens 8-12:** moderate line weight; puzzles introduce genuinely graded difficulty curves (grid size, clue count) across a set rather than a flat difficulty; coloring pages allow a few denser detail zones alongside open space.
- **Teens 13-17:** finer, more even line weight; higher pattern density acceptable (symmetry, repetition) but usually still more open than adult-tier mandala work; puzzles behave like "compact adult" versions — smaller footprint, same rule complexity.
- **Adults:** finest line weight and highest density is expected and often preferred, especially in mandala/botanical coloring — density itself signals "adult-grade" difficulty and time investment. Puzzle books favor tight, efficient grid layouts with minimal decorative line work competing for attention.
- **Senior/large-print:** the exception to the adult density norm — line weight goes back up (bold, uniform) and interior density goes down, prioritizing visual clarity and reduced eye strain over intricacy.

### [Q3-colour]

- **Puzzle books (all ages):** overwhelmingly black-and-white/greyscale interiors; color is reserved for the cover only. Colour inside a puzzle grid is functional at most (e.g., alternating box shading for readability), never decorative.
- **Coloring books (all ages):** interiors are strictly black-and-white line art by definition — colour is deliberately absent so the customer supplies it; the _cover_ alone carries full colour to signal genre and mood.
- **Guided journals/planners:** typically greyscale or one-to-two-colour interiors (black text plus a single restrained accent colour for headers/dividers); full colour interiors are uncommon because they raise print cost without adding functional value.
- **Digital printables:** more likely to use light colour or colour-optional interiors since the customer prints at home and can choose greyscale to save ink; colour here often signals a "decorative" or "kid-facing" printable rather than a strictly utilitarian one.
- Where colour actually carries meaning (as opposed to being decorative): habit-tracker/planner accent colours used consistently to mark categories; cover colour palette signalling genre (calm pastel = mindfulness/journal, high-contrast primary = kids, muted earth tones = nature/adult coloring).

### [Q4-cover-composition]

- **Kids (4-8 to 6-10):** single large friendly focal subject centered or slightly off-center, filling most of the frame; big rounded title lettering, high colour saturation, minimal secondary text (subtitle/age range kept small or omitted).
- **Tweens/Teens:** focal subject smaller relative to frame, more negative space, title treated as a graphic element in its own right (not just sitting on top of art); palette more restrained than the youngest tier.
- **Adult puzzle books:** clean, low-imagery covers — often a simple background texture/pattern plus a bold title treatment; the title and difficulty/volume label are the actual "content" of the cover, since the buyer is shopping on text (title, difficulty, volume number) more than imagery.
- **Adult coloring books:** one intricate but legible sample motif (a single flower, a single mandala) centered on a clean background, functioning as a preview of interior style; title kept compact and placed so it doesn't compete with the artwork.
- **Adult journals/planners:** minimalist — flat colour or subtle texture background, small centered title/wordmark, often no illustrative subject at all; composition signals "calm, uncluttered tool" rather than "product to look at."
- **Senior/large-print:** high-contrast, single clear subject, noticeably larger title text than other adult tiers, and an explicit "Large Print" label treated as a primary (not secondary) piece of cover text since it's a key purchase driver.

### [Q5-palette-mood]

- Kids 4-8 / 6-10: high-contrast primary and secondary hues, bright and saturated, "cheerful/energetic" mood.
- Tweens: slightly desaturated versions of primary/secondary palettes, or single bold accent hue against neutral background — "playful but not babyish" mood.
- Teens: muted or dusty tones (soft blues, sage, blush), sometimes monochrome-plus-one-accent — "calm, personal, low-stimulation" mood.
- Adult mindfulness/gratitude/journal: calm muted pastels or warm neutrals (sand, sage, dusty rose) — "restful, grounding" mood.
- Adult coloring covers: earthy or jewel-toned accents against white/cream background — "cozy, meditative" mood; nature-themed sets often lean "warm autumnal" or "soft botanical."
- Adult puzzle book covers: restrained two-tone or single-accent-on-neutral palettes — "no-nonsense, competent" mood rather than decorative.
- Senior/large-print: high-contrast, clean palettes (e.g., navy/white, forest green/cream) chosen for legibility first, warmth second — mood reads as "clear and reassuring" rather than vibrant.

### [Q6-thumbnail]

- Subject/title must read correctly at a very small square thumbnail: this pushes toward exactly one focal subject (never a busy scene), a title set in a heavy/bold weight sized large relative to the cover, and a limited palette (2-4 colours) so contrast survives compression.
- Text hierarchy at thumbnail size: title > single distinguishing label (volume number, difficulty, "large print") > everything else; subtitles, taglines, and fine print are effectively invisible at thumbnail and should be treated as optional flourishes rather than load-bearing information.
- Background needs strong value contrast against the focal subject and title text (dark-on-light or light-on-dark) since mid-tone-on-mid-tone compositions are the most common thumbnail-legibility failure.
- Series/set covers benefit from a consistent composition template (same subject placement, same title zone) so the whole set reads as a matched series at thumbnail size across a retail grid, differentiated only by a colour or number change. `unverified` that this specifically improves click-through versus more varied covers — a reasonable inference from general grid-browsing behaviour, not a confirmed data point here.
