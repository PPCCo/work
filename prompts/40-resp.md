## PART B

### [01-types] Puzzle/activity types by audience

- **Kids 4-8**: pre-literacy/pre-numeracy formats dominate — dot-to-dot (counting 1-10/1-20), tracing/handwriting, color-by-number with a 4-6 color key, thick-line coloring pages, wide-path mazes, picture-assisted word search (word bank shown as images + text). True crosswords and sudoku are generally too abstract; simplified "picture crosswords" or 4x4 sudoku variants are the entry point instead.
- **Kids 6-10**: this is where core puzzle genres become viable — junior crosswords, word search (themed word lists), word scrambles, simple mazes, 4x4/6x6/9x9 sudoku, and fantasy/animal coloring with moderate line detail. Variant worth adding: "spot the difference" and simple tangram/shape puzzles, both common in this bracket.
- **Tween 8-12**: full logic-puzzle repertoire opens up — logic grid puzzles, cryptograms, word ladders, standard 9x9 sudoku, crosswords with broader vocabulary, and journaling formats begin (light bullet journals, first habit trackers) as tweens start self-directed organization. Mandala coloring shifts toward true geometric complexity.
- **Teen 13-17**: harder tiers of the tween genres (advanced sudoku, lateral-thinking logic, cryptograms) plus a shift toward journaling/self-reflection content — gratitude journals, mindfulness/CBT-style workbooks, habit trackers — reflecting the category's overlap with mental-wellness content for this age (`unverified` — I could not confirm hard sales data segmenting this by age, this is a category-logic inference).
- **Adult**: the broadest catalog — sudoku, crossword, word search, cryptogram, and logic puzzles at all difficulty tiers, plus the largest coloring segment (mandala, nature/botanical, and general adult coloring) and the full planner/journal ecosystem (gratitude, daily planner, habit tracker, bullet journal, mindfulness workbook).
- **Senior Large Print**: same genres as Adult but reformatted — large/bold type, high-contrast print, wider grid spacing — plus two categories that consistently overperform in this bracket specifically: **trivia** (especially decade-nostalgia trivia, e.g. "the 1960s") and **brain games/memory** activity books marketed around cognitive engagement.
- **Additional categories worth adding beyond the original 20**: Trivia Quiz Books, Brain/Memory Games, Word Ladders, Spot-the-Difference, Kakuro/simple arithmetic number puzzles, Tangram/shape puzzles (kids), Line-Art/Affirmation Coloring (adult wellness crossover), Self-Care Journal, Reading Log, Meal Planner.

### [Q2-difficulty] Difficulty tiers and progression

- Common pattern across genres: **one difficulty tier per volume** in a 2-3 book set (Easy / Medium / Hard), rather than ramping difficulty within a single book — this is the dominant convention for sudoku, crossword, and logic-puzzle series in this content plan (`unverified` as an industry-wide statistic, but consistent with how the sets above are structured).
- Within a single non-series book, the more common in-book pattern is a **gentle progression from front to back** (first 10-20% easier, tapering to harder puzzles by the final section), especially for word search, maze, and cryptogram books that aren't split into difficulty-labeled volumes.
- Kids' books (4-8, 6-10) more often use **age/grade-banded tiers** (e.g. "4x4 / 6x6 / 9x9 sudoku" as three separate books) rather than "easy/medium/hard" labeling, since parents are shopping by capability, not abstract difficulty.
- Coloring books generally don't carry a "difficulty" label at all; the equivalent axis is **line complexity** (simple/bold outlines for kids and seniors vs. intricate/fine-line detail for tween-adult mandala and botanical work).
- Journals/planners don't have difficulty tiers; their "progression" equivalent is **structure density** — a teen gratitude journal typically has lighter, more open-ended prompts than an adult 365-day structured journal.

### [Q3-counts] Puzzle counts per book and per page

- Typical puzzle count per single-genre book: roughly **50-100 puzzles** for sudoku/crossword/word search adult and tween books, **30-60** for kids' books (shorter attention span, larger print), and **20-40** for cryptograms and logic-grid puzzles (each one takes longer to solve). These ranges reflect common category norms observed in this space; treat exact counts as `unverified` planning targets, not confirmed benchmarks.
- Standard page layout is **one puzzle per page**, with the answer commonly appearing as a smaller inset on the same page for kids' books, or grouped separately for tween/adult/senior books (see Q5).
- Coloring books commonly run **30-50 designs**, one per page (sometimes with a blank facing page or a thin-paper warning note about bleed-through), and sometimes offer a duplicate/test-color page.
- Journals are structured by **days/weeks** rather than "puzzle count" — a daily gratitude journal is typically 90 or 365 entries; a weekly habit tracker book runs 12-26 weekly spreads.
- Large-print senior books usually **reduce total puzzle count** relative to the standard adult equivalent (e.g. 60-80 instead of 100) to accommodate bigger grids and type without inflating page count excessively.

### [04-page-budget] Page budget and content mix

- Typical total book length in this space clusters around **100-120 pages** for puzzle books and **50-100 pages** for coloring books (`unverified` general norm, common print-on-demand sweet spot for cost/perceived value).
- Front matter usually includes: title/copyright page, a short "how to solve" or "how to use this book" page (especially for sudoku/logic/cryptogram genres), and sometimes a table of contents when the book is divided into themed sections.
- Back matter: **answer key section** (see Q5) and, increasingly common, a call-to-action page ("if you enjoyed this book, check out Book 2 in the series" / review request page).
- Large-print senior books allocate more pages per unit of content (bigger grids, wider margins), so their page count is comparable to the adult equivalent while containing fewer total puzzles.
- Journals/planners spend most of the page budget on the recurring template itself, with a short front-matter intro (how to use the journal) and occasionally a closing reflection page.

### [Q5-answer-keys] Answer-key conventions

- The dominant convention for sudoku, crossword, word search, and logic puzzles is **answers grouped in a back section**, ordered to match puzzle numbering, rather than inline per-page — this keeps the puzzle pages clean for the solver and is the most common layout across this genre space.
- Cryptograms sometimes place the answer directly below the puzzle grid rather than in the back, since the "aha" of a cryptogram is often checked immediately.
- Kids' books (4-8, 6-10) more frequently use **inline or same-page answers** (small, easy to skip) since young readers/parents want quick verification without flipping pages.
- Buyers in this category broadly expect: **every sudoku and logic puzzle has a unique solution**, mazes have exactly one correct path (unless explicitly marketed as multi-path), and word searches don't have overlapping/ambiguous word placements. Meeting this bar generally requires generating puzzles with a solver/validator rather than hand-placing them, to avoid the most common one-star complaint in this category (duplicate puzzles, unsolvable grids, or answer-key mismatches) — this complaint pattern is a well-known category risk, but I have not verified it against a specific dataset of reviews (`unverified`).
- Coloring books and journals have no answer-key requirement.

### [Q6-series-arc] Multi-book series variation

- **Difficulty-tiered series** (most sudoku/crossword/logic sets above): each volume keeps identical layout, page count, and puzzle count, varying only the difficulty tier — this is the simplest arc to produce consistently and the one used for most sets in Part A.
- **Theme-tiered series** (word search, coloring, trivia): each volume keeps a consistent difficulty level but rotates subject matter (e.g. Animals → Space → Ocean), which supports collectability without forcing a harder/easier read.
- **Hybrid arcs** (e.g. Kids Sudoku Starter, Large Print Number Puzzles): grid size or content type itself changes per volume (4x4 → 6x6 → 9x9), functioning as both a difficulty and format progression — this suits sudoku, mazes, and number puzzles where the format has a natural size/complexity ladder.
- Across all arc types, keeping the **cover design language, trim size, and interior template consistent** across a set is the norm buyers expect from a "Volume 1/2/3" series, so the differentiator should live in content (theme or difficulty) rather than layout.
- For single-book categories in this plan (e.g. most teen/adult journals), there is no natural series arc unless the publisher chooses to spin off theme variants later (e.g. a second gratitude journal themed around a new year or seasonal reset).
