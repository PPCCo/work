# KDP to website conversion


Q: It seems like there is not a lot of profit if I publish printable colouring books on platforms like Etsy and KDP because of low profit margins and high competition. Would it be better to create my own niche website and do paid and free marketing on different platforms like instagram, twitter, facebook.

A: A niche website on its own won't fix this. It changes where your costs sit, not whether they exist.

**What a website changes**
- You keep about 97% of each sale (card fees only) instead of 85–88% on Etsy, and you own the customer email list.
- You lose Etsy's built-in buyer traffic and have to pay for every visitor yourself.

**Why paid social is hard for cheap printables.** The figures below are my own estimates, not sourced data. Say a $12 bundle nets about $11.30 after card fees. Cold social traffic typically converts to a purchase at about 1–2%. If Instagram or Facebook clicks cost around $0.50–$1.00, you'd pay roughly $25–$100 to acquire one buyer of an $11 product. That loses money on the first sale. The same break-even maths applied to Etsy Ads in the plan, where it was already marginal.

**Channels**
- **Twitter/X** is a poor fit for this audience. Skip it.
- **Pinterest** is the best fit, and it's the one the plan already recommends. People search it for printable and craft projects, and pins keep working for months.
- **Instagram, Facebook and TikTok** work for organic content such as time-lapses, flip-throughs and finished-page showcases. Facebook groups for seniors, parents and hobbyists can also work.

**When a website makes sense.** It pays off when each customer is worth much more than one $8 purchase. That means:
1. **Recurring revenue.** A membership with a new themed pack each month or season, for example $6–9 a month. This is the biggest change, because it turns one-time buyers into recurring ones.
2. **Larger baskets.** Seasonal bundles and "all-year" packs at $20–40 rather than single files.
3. **A warm audience first.** Build an email list from free sample packs and reach it with free content. Email is the only channel where paid-style economics work, because the traffic is already yours.
4. **Selling to groups.** Teachers, aged-care activity coordinators, therapists, churches and homeschool groups buy many copies and license for group use. This is a hypothesis I haven't researched. It could be a better fit for large-print puzzles than selling to individual consumers.

**What I'd do instead of choosing one**
- Keep Etsy and KDP as discovery channels. They bring in buyers without ad spend.
- Open a small own-site shop (Payhip or Shopify) for the membership, bundles and email list.
- Run a 90-day test with one niche and a fixed budget, and decide by data. Etsy's rules restrict steering buyers off the platform, so check the current wording before putting site links in listings or delivery files.
- Decision rule: if you can't get email signups for under about $1–2 each, or can't get 20 or more paying members, the website path doesn't work and you cut it.

I can set up that 90-day test as a concrete plan with budget, content calendar and gates. I could also rework the financial scenarios in the business plan to include a membership model, if you want.

Q: is it possible, to make a website but not advertize at all, but instead make bundle offers or some other incentives to get those buyers from Etsy and KDP to email subscribe to my website

A: Yes, this is a well-used approach: sell on Etsy and KDP, and use the product to move buyers onto your own email list. It costs no ad spend, only a little design time. The catch is that both platforms have rules, and I haven't checked their current wording, so verify before relying on any of this.

**What usually works**
- **Bonus content with an opt-in.** Put a "free bonus pages" offer inside every product. Examples are 10 extra coloring pages, the answer key for a puzzle book, a large-print version, or a matching seasonal pack. The buyer scans a QR code or visits a short URL, then enters their email to get it.
- **Offer something the platform doesn't provide.** Good candidates are a monthly free page, early access to new seasonal packs, a members-only discount on bundles, and a printable "coloring challenge" calendar.
- **Etsy digital files.** The delivered PDF is the best place for the offer, for example on a final "thank you and bonus" page. Etsy buyers have already paid, so the offer is a genuine extra.
- **KDP books.** Put the QR code and short URL on the last page or the back matter. Authors do this routinely with reader magnets, but check KDP's content guidelines first, because they restrict some promotional and external-link content.

**Rules to check**
- **Etsy:** It restricts steering buyers off-platform to complete purchases and restricts marketing messages sent through its own messaging. A bonus-page link inside the file is common practice, but I haven't verified the current policy text.
- **KDP:** Check the rules on links and QR codes in book interiors. Don't offer anything in exchange for reviews.
- **Consent law:** The signup must be voluntary and clearly say what they'll receive. Don't make it a condition of delivering what they bought, and always include an unsubscribe link. Australia's Spam Act 2003, CAN-SPAM and GDPR all apply depending on where subscribers live.

**Expect modest numbers.** My rough guess, not sourced data, is that a bonus offer inside a product converts a low single-digit percentage of buyers. At 3% and 50 sales a month, that's about 1–2 new subscribers a day at best, and less at first. So it's a slow build, which is the cost of spending nothing.

**What the list needs to be worth anything**
1. A free email tool (most have a free tier) and a simple landing page.
2. A welcome sequence of about 3–5 emails that delivers the bonus, shows related products, and asks one question about their interests, so you can segment.
3. A reason to stay subscribed: a new free page or puzzle monthly, plus early seasonal launches.
4. A paid offer on your own site: bundles, a membership, or direct sales, where you keep about 97% of each sale.

**A test to run first.** Add the bonus page to your 20–30 Christmas printables and 3–5 KDP books. After 60 days, measure sales, signups and signup rate per product. If a product gets at least 3% signups, copy that offer to the rest of the catalog. If none do, change the offer before adding more products.
